LECTURE 01

📝 Technical Writing: Definition and Purpose

What is technical writing?

Technical writing is the process of explaining specialized information in a clear, accurate, organized, and useful way. It helps a specific audience understand a topic, use a product, follow a procedure, or make a decision. Technical writing is not limited to computers or engineering; it is used whenever people need reliable instructions or explanations.

Purpose and importance

The main purpose is to help readers understand information or complete a task correctly. A good technical document saves time, reduces confusion, supports safe work, and gives readers information they can use. For example, a printer manual explains setup and troubleshooting, while a project report explains what was done and what was discovered.

Characteristics of good technical writing

CharacteristicMeaningExample
ClarityThe message is easy to understand.“Click Save to store the file.”
AccuracyFacts and instructions are correct.Use the correct voltage listed by the manufacturer.
ConcisenessUnnecessary words are removed.“Use the button” instead of “Make use of the button.”
ObjectivityInformation is based on facts, not unsupported opinions.Report measured results rather than saying a product is “the best.”
Audience awarenessLanguage and detail fit the reader.A beginner's guide explains unfamiliar terms.
Logical organizationIdeas appear in a useful order.Give setup steps before troubleshooting steps.

Know your audience

Before writing, consider who will read the document, what they already know, what they need to do, and what terms they understand. A guide for new users should explain each step. A document for experienced technicians may use accepted technical terms but should still be clear.

Where is technical writing used?

  • Education: research papers, laboratory procedures, project reports.
  • Business: proposals, memos, process documents, reports.
  • Technology: software documentation, installation guides, help pages.
  • Healthcare: patient instructions and equipment procedures.
  • Industry: safety procedures, specifications, maintenance manuals.

Common examples

User manuals explain how to operate a product. Instructional guides list steps for a task. Technical reports present methods and findings. Proposals recommend a plan. Standard operating procedures explain how to carry out recurring work consistently.

Technical writing vs. creative writing

Technical writingCreative writing
Usually prioritizes clear information and task completion.Often prioritizes imagination, emotion, and storytelling.
Uses evidence, instructions, and structured explanations.May use figurative language and invented characters or events.
Example: how to install an application.Example: a short story about an application that comes alive.
Before: “The thing should be done after the button thing is pressed.”
Improved: “Click Submit to send the form.”
The improved instruction names the action and the result clearly.
Remember: Good technical writing puts the reader's needs first. Clear wording, correct information, and logical order work together.

Practice activity

Rewrite this instruction so a beginner can understand it: “Do the necessary procedure with the device after activation.”

Suggested answer

“Turn on the device, then follow the setup instructions on the screen.” The exact answer may vary, but it should name the action and avoid vague wording.

LECTURE 02

🩷 The ABCs of Technical Writing

The ABCs are a simple reminder of three important principles: Accuracy, Brevity, and Clarity. They help writers create information readers can trust and understand.

A — Accuracy 🎯

Accuracy means that information is correct, complete enough for the task, and supported by reliable facts. Check names, dates, calculations, measurements, labels, and instructions before publishing. When using data from another source, record where it came from and do not change its meaning.

Inaccurate: “The meeting is on Friday, May 12,” when the calendar shows May 13.
Accurate: “The meeting is on Saturday, May 13.”
Always verify dates and details before sharing.

B — Brevity ✂️

Brevity means using only the words needed to communicate the message. It does not mean removing important information. Remove repeated ideas, unnecessary introductions, and wordy phrases while keeping the meaning complete.

Wordy versionConcise version
“At this point in time, please make use of the Save button.”“Click Save now.”
“Due to the fact that the file is large, uploading takes longer.”“Because the file is large, uploading takes longer.”
“The system has the ability to create reports.”“The system can create reports.”

C — Clarity 💡

Clarity means readers can understand the message without guessing. Use familiar words, specific actions, short paragraphs, descriptive headings, and a logical sequence. Explain technical terms when the audience may not know them. Avoid vague words such as “thing,” “soon,” or “appropriate” when exact details are needed.

Unclear: “Put it there and do the process.”
Clear: “Place the paper in Tray 1, then press Print.”

How the ABCs work together

PrincipleAsk yourselfEditing check
AccuracyIs it correct?Verify facts and test instructions.
BrevityCan I say it in fewer words?Remove repetition without losing meaning.
ClarityWill the reader understand it?Replace vague wording and explain needed terms.

Before-and-after examples

  1. Before: “The user should proceed to make a selection of the button labeled Login.” After: “Select Login.” (Brevity)
  2. Before: “The application is very good.” After: “The application saves each submitted form to the database.” (Accuracy and specificity)
  3. Before: “Move the item to the other place.” After: “Move the file to the Documents folder.” (Clarity)
Editing trick: Read your writing aloud. Check each statement for correctness, remove unnecessary words, and ask whether a new reader would understand it.

Practice activity

Improve this sentence: “In the event that the user has forgotten the password, it is possible for them to make use of the password reset feature.”

Suggested answer

“If users forget their passwords, they can use the password reset feature.” It keeps the meaning while improving brevity and clarity.

LECTURE 03

📒 Outlining the Subject

What is an outline?

An outline is a plan that arranges a topic's main ideas and supporting details before the writer drafts the full document. It acts like a map: it helps the writer stay focused, avoid repeating ideas, and put information in a logical order.

Steps for making an outline

  1. Identify the topic: State what the document will discuss.
  2. Set the purpose: Decide whether you will explain, instruct, compare, or recommend.
  3. Know the audience: Consider what readers already know and what they need.
  4. Gather information: Collect relevant facts, examples, and sources.
  5. Group related ideas: Place similar points under the same heading.
  6. Arrange ideas: Use a sequence that fits the topic, such as chronological, general-to-specific, or problem-to-solution.
  7. Review: Check that each point supports the main topic and that no key step is missing.

Types and formats

A topic outline uses short words or phrases. A sentence outline uses complete sentences to state each idea more fully. An alphanumeric outline uses Roman numerals, capital letters, Arabic numerals, and lowercase letters. A decimal outline uses numbers such as 1, 1.1, and 1.1.1.

Topic outline example:
I. Introduction
II. Preparation
III. Printing steps
IV. Troubleshooting
V. Conclusion

Sentence outline example:
I. This guide explains how a beginner can print a document.
II. The user should check the printer, ink, paper, and connection before printing.

Sample outline: How to Use a Printer

  1. Introduction
    1. Purpose of the guide
    2. Intended users
  2. Printer Preparation
    1. Connect the printer
    2. Check or install ink
    3. Load paper
  3. Printing a Document
    1. Open the document
    2. Select print settings
    3. Print the document
  4. Troubleshooting
    1. Clear a paper jam according to the manual
    2. Check ink and print quality
  5. Conclusion
    1. Review the main steps
    2. Remind readers to follow printer safety guidance

The introduction tells readers what the guide covers. Preparation ensures the device is ready. The main steps explain the task in order. Troubleshooting addresses common problems. The conclusion briefly reinforces the process.

Short outline for a technical report

  1. Title and purpose
  2. Background or problem
  3. Method or procedure
  4. Results and observations
  5. Discussion
  6. Conclusion and recommendations
  7. References and appendices, when needed

Useful outlining tips

  • Use parallel wording for headings at the same level.
  • Do not create a subheading with only one child point; either add another point or combine them.
  • Keep each point relevant to the main topic.
  • For instructions, arrange steps in the order the reader must perform them.

Practice activity

Arrange these ideas into a sensible outline for “Sending an Email”: write the message, open the email app, enter the recipient, review the message, click Send.

Suggested answer
  1. Open the email app.
  2. Enter the recipient's email address.
  3. Write the message and subject.
  4. Review the message and recipient.
  5. Click Send.

This order follows the actual process and includes a check before sending.

LECTURE 04

📑 Types of Technical Papers

Technical papers are documents used to explain information, report findings, recommend actions, or guide readers through a process. The best type depends on the purpose, audience, and information needed.

Common types of technical documents

TypePurpose and audienceTypical parts / example
Technical reportRecords a problem, method, findings, or recommendations for managers, clients, or a project team.Title, purpose, method, results, discussion, conclusion. Example: “Inventory System Testing Report.”
Research paperInvestigates a question and presents evidence for an academic or professional audience.Abstract, introduction, literature review, method, results, discussion, references. Example: a study of online learning tools.
Project proposalPersuades a decision-maker to approve a plan, project, or budget.Problem, objectives, scope, method, timeline, budget, expected results. Example: proposal for a barangay reservation website.
User manualExplains how to use a product or system.Overview, requirements, setup, instructions, troubleshooting. Example: guide to using an online store.
Instructional guide / how-toShows readers how to complete a task in sequence.Materials, numbered steps, warnings, result. Example: how to reset a password.
Business letterCommunicates formally with a person or organization.Addresses, date, salutation, body, closing, signature. Example: request for project information.
Memorandum (memo)Shares internal announcements, policies, or updates in an organization.To, From, Date, Subject, message. Example: notice of a scheduled system update.
Feasibility studyEvaluates whether a proposed project is practical.Technical, operational, financial, and schedule considerations. Example: assessing whether a small shop can adopt a POS system.
Progress reportShows work completed, work remaining, issues, and next steps.Summary, completed tasks, problems, schedule, next actions. Example: monthly capstone update.
Technical specificationsStates precise requirements or characteristics a product or system must meet.Performance, materials, dimensions, functions, standards. Example: minimum browser and server requirements.

How four common documents differ

📋 Report

Explains what happened, how it was examined, and what the findings mean.

🔬 Research paper

Answers a research question using a systematic method and evidence.

💡 Proposal

Recommends what should be done and why it should be approved.

Quick distinction: A manual helps someone use something. A proposal asks for approval. A report records and explains findings. A research paper investigates a question.

Short sample excerpts

Report: “During testing, the system correctly recorded 18 of 20 sample reservations. Two tests revealed an error in date validation.”
Proposal: “The project proposes an online reservation portal to reduce duplicate bookings and make facility schedules easier to check.”
Manual: “To submit a reservation, sign in, choose a facility, select an available date, complete the form, and click Submit.”
Memo: “To: All Staff | From: System Administrator | Subject: Scheduled Maintenance. The system will be unavailable from 8:00–9:00 PM on Friday.”

Practice activity

Choose the most suitable document: (1) You want permission to build a new website. (2) You need to explain how to reset a password. (3) You need to report the results of system testing. (4) You need to tell staff about a maintenance schedule.

Answer key
  1. Project proposal — requests approval for a plan.
  2. User manual or instructional guide — explains steps.
  3. Technical report — presents testing methods and findings.
  4. Memo — communicates an internal announcement.

LECTURE 05

✏️ The Basic English Structure

Good technical writing uses grammatically correct sentences so readers can follow instructions and understand information. A complete sentence normally expresses a complete thought and contains a subject and a predicate.

Parts of a sentence

PartFunctionExample
SubjectWho or what the sentence is about.The technician repairs the computer.
VerbShows an action or state.The technician repairs the computer.
ObjectReceives the action of a verb.The technician repairs the computer.
PredicateWhat is said about the subject; includes the verb.“repairs the computer”
ComplementCompletes the meaning of a linking verb or describes an object.The application is reliable.

Five common sentence patterns

  1. Subject + Verb (S + V): “The server runs.”
  2. Subject + Verb + Object (S + V + O): “The system stores records.”
  3. Subject + Linking Verb + Complement: “The application is reliable.”
  4. Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object: “The administrator sent the staff a report.” (“staff” = indirect object; “report” = direct object.)
  5. Subject + Verb + Object + Object Complement: “The team found the system efficient.” (“system” = object; “efficient” describes the object.)

Four sentence types by structure

  • Simple: one independent clause. “The user saves the file.”
  • Compound: two independent clauses joined correctly. “The user saves the file, and the system confirms it.”
  • Complex: one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. “When the upload finishes, the system displays a message.”
  • Compound-complex: at least two independent clauses and one dependent clause. “When the upload finishes, the system displays a message, and the user receives a notification.”

Parts of speech

Noun names a person, place, thing, or idea; pronoun replaces a noun; verb expresses action or state; adjective describes a noun; adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb; preposition shows a relationship; conjunction connects words or clauses; and an interjection expresses a sudden feeling. Technical documents commonly rely on precise nouns and verbs.

Subject-verb agreement

A singular subject generally takes a singular verb in the present tense; a plural subject takes a plural verb. Example: “The system stores data.” “The systems store data.” Check the real subject, especially when extra phrases appear between the subject and verb.

Active and passive voice

Active: “The administrator updates the schedule.” The subject performs the action, so the sentence is direct.
Passive: “The schedule is updated by the administrator.” The focus is on the schedule or the action. Passive voice can be useful when the actor is unknown or less important, but active voice is often clearer for instructions.

Punctuation, capitalization, and parallel structure

  • Begin sentences with a capital letter and end them with appropriate punctuation.
  • Use commas to separate items in a list and to prevent confusion.
  • Keep list items grammatically parallel: “Create an account, verify your email, and sign in.”
  • Use consistent verb forms in instructions, usually direct commands: “Open the file. Select Print. Choose a printer.”

Common errors and corrections

IncorrectCorrectReason
The system store records.The system stores records.Singular subject needs “stores.”
Click save to Save the fileClick Save to save the file.Correct capitalization and punctuation.
The user can entering a password.The user can enter a password.Use the base verb after “can.”
Open the app, entering your email, and click Login.Open the app, enter your email, and click Login.Use parallel verb forms.

Practice activity

Correct this sentence: “The users opens the website and entering their password.”

Suggested answer

“The users open the website and enter their passwords.” The plural subject “users” takes “open,” and the verbs “open” and “enter” are parallel.

LECTURE 06

💌 Parts of a Letter

A formal or business letter is used to communicate professionally with an individual or organization. It may request information, make a complaint, apply for a position, give an official response, or share important information.

Parts of a formal letter

  1. Heading / sender's address: The writer's address or organization details, if needed.
  2. Date: The date the letter is written.
  3. Inside address: The recipient's name, title, organization, and address.
  4. Salutation: A polite greeting, such as “Dear Ms. Santos:”
  5. Subject line (optional): A short statement of the letter's purpose.
  6. Body: The main message, usually organized into an opening, details, and closing request or next step.
  7. Complimentary close: A professional ending, such as “Sincerely,”
  8. Signature: The writer's handwritten or approved digital signature when required.
  9. Typed name and position: Identifies the writer.
  10. Enclosure / attachment note (if applicable): Identifies documents included with the letter.

Sample formal letter with labels

HEADING
BSIS-3A, City College of Calapan
Calapan City, Oriental Mindoro

DATE
October 10, 2026

INSIDE ADDRESS
Ms. Maria Santos
Information Systems Coordinator
Sample Learning Center
Calapan City

SALUTATION
Dear Ms. Santos:

SUBJECT
Request for Information for a School Project

BODY — OPENING
I am a third-year Information Systems student conducting a school project about the use of digital systems in daily operations.

BODY — DETAILS
I would like to ask whether your office could share general information about the processes involved in managing records and preparing reports. The information will be used for academic purposes only, and any shared details will be handled respectfully.

BODY — CLOSING
Thank you for considering my request. I would appreciate any information or guidance you can provide.

COMPLIMENTARY CLOSE
Sincerely,

SIGNATURE AND TYPED NAME

Loreen Jane Claud Vinas
BSIS-3A Student

ENCLOSURE (ONLY IF INCLUDED)
Enclosure: Project information sheet

Block format

In full-block format, the main parts of the letter begin at the left margin, with blank lines separating sections. It is easy to scan and commonly used for business correspondence. Keep the tone polite, direct, and professional. Use short paragraphs and explain the request clearly.

Formal vs. informal letters

Formal letterInformal letter
Used for professional, school, or official purposes.Used for friends, family, or casual personal communication.
Uses respectful language and a clear structure.May use casual language and flexible formatting.
Example: request for information from an office.Example: a note to a friend.

Basic professional email etiquette

  • Write a specific subject line.
  • Use a respectful greeting.
  • State your purpose early and keep the message organized.
  • Check spelling, names, attachments, and recipient addresses before sending.
  • Use a professional email address and close politely.
  • Do not use all capital letters or send sensitive information unnecessarily.

Common mistakes

Avoid missing dates, incorrect recipient names, overly casual greetings, unclear requests, long blocks of text, spelling mistakes, and forgetting attachments mentioned in the letter.

Answer key

  2. Date
  3. Inside address
  4. Salutation
  5. Body
  6. Complimentary close
  7. Signature and typed name

LECTURE 07

📊 Graphs and Charts

Graphs and charts turn data into visual forms so readers can compare values, see patterns, understand proportions, and make informed decisions. Choose a visual that fits the question and show the data honestly.

Tables, graphs, and charts

A table is useful when readers need exact values. A graph helps show comparisons or trends. A chart is a broad term for a visual representation of information, including pie charts and flowcharts.

Essential elements

  • Title: tells readers what the visual shows.
  • Labels: name categories, axes, or data series.
  • Axes and units: show the scale and what is being measured.
  • Legend: explains colors or symbols when multiple series are shown.
  • Data source: identifies where the information came from, when applicable.

Other visual formats

Choosing the right visual

  • Use a bar graph to compare categories.
  • Use a line graph to show trends over time.
  • Use a pie chart to show parts of a whole when there are only a few categories.
  • Use a table when readers need exact values.
  • Use a flowchart for steps or decisions, not numerical comparisons.

Avoid misleading visuals

Label axes and units. Use consistent scales and do not crop an axis in a way that exaggerates differences without clearly indicating it. Do not use 3D effects that make values hard to compare. Make sure percentages and totals are calculated correctly. Cite the source of real data and clearly label estimates or sample data.

  2. Line graph — shows change over time.
  3. Pie chart — shows parts of a whole.
  4. Flowchart — shows a process and possible decisions.
